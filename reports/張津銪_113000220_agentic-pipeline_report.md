## 3. Feature filtering and term correlations

**Variance filter** (`variance_11`, population variance, threshold 0.1) kept 807 of 5,000 terms. The highest-variance terms were *jpeg* (25.14), *edu* (16.48), *image* (13.50), *god* (11.91), *com* (7.60), *jesus* (7.34) and *graphics* (6.42). *jpeg* has the highest variance even though it is not among the 20 most frequent terms. That pattern means a term concentrated heavily in a few documents, and variance on raw counts rewards such terms. No sci.med term reached the top 10. The cut-off also removed meaningful words just below 0.1 (*prevention*, *rendering*, *passage*, *truths*), so 0.1 is too strict for topic analysis.

**Correlation with sci.med (one-vs-rest).** The Pearson filter (`pearson_12`, |r| ≥ 0.1) kept 268 terms, and **all top 10 belong to a single poster's signature**: *pitt* (0.380), *gordon*, *geb*, *banks*, *n3jxp*, *chastity*, *intellect*, *cadre*, *dsl*, *shameful*. These come from an address (geb@cadre.dsl.pitt.edu) and a sign-off quote ("Skepticism is the chastity of the intellect…"). This is label leakage: the terms identify *who* posts in sci.med, not *what* sci.med is about. Several of them share identical values (*chastity* = *intellect* = 0.317415; *cadre* = *dsl* = *shameful* = 0.316571), so they always co-occur and are fully redundant. The Spearman filter (`spearman_13`) kept more terms (390). It brought real medical words into the top 10 (*disease* 0.320, *medical* 0.319) and a strong negative one (*god* −0.319). *n3jxp*, *chastity* and *intellect* have exactly the same value under Pearson and Spearman. My explanation is that a word occurring exactly once per post gives a binary 0/1 column, and ranking a binary column is a linear transform, so the two coefficients coincide. In both filters the terms removed just below 0.1 were specific medical words with tied values (Spearman: *asthma*, *pills*, *intestinal*, *phenylalanine*, all 0.099794). Correlation filtering penalises words that are perfectly specific to a class but rare. The bar chart of `spearman_13` summarises this: 10 of the top 15 terms are signature words, 4 are medical (*disease*, *medical*, *doctor*, *medicine*) and *god* is the only negative bar.

![Spearman correlation with sci.med](../plots/張津銪_agentic-pipeline_plot_turn80.png)

The term-term correlation matrix of the 20 highest-variance terms (`feature_corr_14`) shows a very tight graphics cluster: *jpeg*–*gif* 0.96, *jpeg*–*file* 0.90, *image*–*images* 0.89. These terms are largely redundant and probably come from the same few long format-FAQ posts. *graphics*–*pub* is 0.92, where *pub* is part of FTP paths such as /pub/graphics. This suggests that part of *graphics*' variance comes from FTP site listings rather than discussion. The religion terms are weakly linked (*god*–*jesus* only 0.23, *god*–*atheists* 0.40), consistent with *god* being used in both alt.atheism and soc.religion.christian.

## 4. Frequent pattern mining (sci.med)

I mined sci.med with three settings. The pattern tool builds its own stop-word-filtered vectorizer per category, independent of `build_dtm_8`. With the term-frequency filter and FP-Growth at min_sup = 9 (`patterns_15`; 15,170 terms kept, 5,422 patterns), the top patterns were generic single words (*advance*, *easy*, *mass*, support ≤ 23). This filter keeps only the 5th–95th frequency percentiles, so it cut the most frequent sci.med words, which is exactly where the topic vocabulary sits. MaxFP-Growth with the same settings (`patterns_16`) returned 859 maximal itemsets. 4,563 frequent itemsets were therefore subsets of larger ones, which proves that multi-word patterns exist, but all of them have lower support than the single words shown in the top 20. With the TF-IDF filter and min_sup = 80 (`patterns_17`; 12,807 terms, 8,495 patterns), there is no upper cut, and the header words took over: *subject* appears in all 594 sci.med posts, *lines* in 585, *organization* in 582, and {subject, edu, organization, lines} in 400. *writes* (344) and *article* (343) show that over half the posts are quoted replies. Across the three runs, no filter setting surfaced medical topics, because the headers had not been removed.

## 5. Dimensionality reduction and document similarity

PCA on `build_dtm_8` (`reduce_dims_18`) explained 16.17% + 7.39% = 23.56% of the variance with two components. This high share is a warning rather than a success. Four comp.graphics documents lie far from everything else (up to about Dim1 ≈ 300), while the other documents collapse near the origin. I suspect these are the four 47k–60k-character posts from `describe_data_5`, but the tools cannot map points back to documents, so this link is not verified. UMAP (`reduce_dims_19`, n_neighbors = 15) preserves local neighbourhoods and is not dominated by those outliers. It separates soc.religion.christian (upper left) from alt.atheism (lower left), with mixing only at the border. comp.graphics and sci.med overlap heavily in the middle, and a small, tight sci.med island sits far away near (8, 2.7). One hypothesis is that this island is the signature-sharing posts found in `pearson_12`, but I could not confirm it. `binarize_20` produced a (2257, 4) one-hot label matrix whose class counts exactly match the loading step; its sci.med column is the one-vs-rest target used by the correlation filters. Cosine similarity was 0.088 for two comp.graphics posts (`cosine_sim_21`, docs 0 and 1: printer image conversion vs. mesh trimming) and 0.039 for a comp.graphics vs. soc.religion.christian pair (`cosine_sim_22`, docs 0 and 3). The same-category pair scores higher, but both values are low, and two pairs are only an illustration, not evidence.

## 6. Conclusion and limitations

The main finding is that, with raw counts, this dataset is shaped more by **Usenet formatting and individual posters than by topic**. Header and address words top the frequency list and every TF-IDF pattern. One author's signature dominates the sci.med correlations. A handful of long comp.graphics FAQ-style posts drive the variance filter, the term-correlation clusters and the PCA. The topic signal is still there: Spearman finds *disease* and *medical*, and UMAP separates the religion groups. It is weak, though, and easy to confuse with these artefacts. In a second iteration I would strip headers, quoted reply lines and signatures, and drop pure number tokens, before building the DTM. I would then compare TF-IDF weighting or length normalisation against raw counts. Limitations of this run: the tool summaries show only the top 10–20 items of each result, so some interpretations (for example, which documents form the PCA outliers or the UMAP island) remain hypotheses. The variance threshold (0.1) and correlation threshold (0.1) were each tried at a single value.
